I think a lot of us were surprised by the results of last night's free dance competition. Looking at the scoring breakdowns for each of the judges it's hard not to be a bit suspicious that something was going on behind the scenes. I am going to try to create a metric to measure how much the judges disagree so that I can compare this years results with the past ten years of ice dance results. Once the rest of the events are finished I can also compare with other events.

## Defining the Chaos/Bias Metric using Skater Scores
In order to understand the numbers, we must understand the metric. In a sport without judging (e.g. long jump), athlete $i$'s score can be represented like this, where $S_i$ is the score that athlete $i$ earns, $\mu$ is the average score across all competitors and $A_i$ is a measure of how well athlete $i$ performed in that event compared to other athletes.

$$S_i = \mu + A_i$$

For my metric I wanted to normalize for a judge's overall strictness or leniency. If Judge A's scores are 3 points lower than Judge B's scores for every skater that should not factor into the metric. To introduce this effect, we change the formula to this where $J_j$ represents how strict or lenient judge $j$ is compared to other judges across all athletes.

$$S_{i,j} = \mu + A_i + J_j$$  

In an ideal world, where all the judges are perfectly fair, this formula can be used to generate score given to each athlete by each judge and we can define $A_i$ and $J_j$ as below where $\bar{S_i}$ is the average of the scores that athlete $i$ received and $\bar{S_j}$ is the average of the scores that judge $j$ gave.

$$A_i = \bar{S_{i}} - \mu \quad J_j = \bar{S_{j}} - \mu$$

If we use this formula as the expected score given to athlete $i$ by judge $j$ then we can compare the expected score to the actual score to measure the deviation $R$. 

$$R_{i,J} = S_{i,j} - (\mu - A_i - J_j)$$

This difference can be useful however because we only have one datapoint and it does not take into account if an event generally has larger differentials, I prefer to look at the competition as a whole. We can combine the differences or residuals into a single metric $B$ like this which we can compare across competititons.

$$ B = \frac{\sum_{i,j}{R_{i,j}^2}}{\sum_{i,j}{\left(S_{i,j} - \mu\right)^2}} $$

### Using Raw Scores vs Ordinals
I believe that looking at the raw scores is more useful than the ordinals because the scores can be so close between some pairs. For example, the Italian judge ranked the Czech teams in opposite order. However both of the scores given were very similar to each other and similar to the final scores recieved by both teams so I don't believe this mismatch should be an indication of bias in the same way that the French judge's scoring of Fournier-Beaudry/Cizeron and Chock/Bates. I think ordinals are a good way to quickly see how much disagreement there is, but scores are more meaningful.

## Conclusions and Future Work
This analysis only compares whether the judges on the panel agree with each other. If all the judges collectively decide to rate one team higher than they should it will not be captured here. 

Overall I think transparency in the judging is a double edged sword. On the one hand naming the judges lets the viewers see where potential bias comes frome but on the other hand if you are a judge and your federation is putting pressure on you to do something sketchy they have a way to check whether you came through or not.
